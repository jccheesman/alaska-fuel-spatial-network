Blind Cost Derivations: Alaska Bulk Fuel Delivery by Mode

Prepared: July 15, 2026 (derivations + primary-source verification round) Purpose: Independent, open-web derivation of transport cost in USD per gallon-mile (2026 dollars) for each fuel-delivery mode in the DOE MAS fuel-logistics routing model: highway truck, marine barge, aircraft, and winter ice road / snow trail.

Methodology

Each mode was assigned to a separate research agent operating blind: no access to the model's current cost values, to the project's prior derivations, or to each other's work — so no estimate could anchor on an existing number. Each agent was required to:

  1. Use at least three genuinely independent methods (published tariffs/contracts, engineering cost build-up, and revealed delivered-price differentials from state fuel-price surveys and contracts);
  2. Keep a derivation log recording every search, source acceptance/rejection, and assumption made at each decision point;
  3. Show all arithmetic and state all inflation/currency conversions;
  4. Report a point estimate, range, confidence, and an explicit statement of what the figure includes and excludes.

Convergence between independent methods — not any single source — is the evidence standard.

Cross-cutting findings

1. Fixed per-delivery costs dominate short hauls in every mode. Every derivation independently found a two-part cost structure — a true per-mile rate plus a fixed per-gallon cost per delivery event (truck: ~$0.20/gal distributor/drop fixed; barge: $0.15–0.30/gal hub transfer plus ~$1.50/gal village lightering; plane: ~$2.20/gal per air delivery; ice road: load/unload and mobilization). A flat $/gal-mi therefore systematically underprices short hops and overprices long hauls. Where the model keeps single per-mile rates, the point estimates above are calibrated to each mode's typical route length; the fixed components belong in intermodal transfer fees.

2. Mode ordering is economically coherent. Ocean barge (0.00026) < road (0.0007) < ice road (0.010) < air (0.025) — each step roughly 3–40× the previous — which reproduces observed behavior: communities barge everything they can, truck what the road system reaches, build winter roads to displace airlift, and fly only what they must.

3. The mid-2026 fuel price shock is a commodity effect, not a freight effect. The June 2026 (Iran/Hormuz) price spike moved delivered fuel prices by dollars per gallon, but the estimated freight pass-through is small (e.g., ≈ +$0.02/gal on a barge voyage). All rates here are calibrated to pre-shock (Jan–early-2026) price relationships and are stated in real 2026 dollars.

4. Retail price differentials overstate freight. In every revealed-price method, the majority of a remote community's price premium proved to be storage, working capital, and retail margin rather than transport (e.g., ~85–90% of Bethel's $2.78/gal net premium is non-freight). The derivations use differentials only where the non-freight components could be netted out or bounded.

5. Ice road remains the widest-uncertainty mode. No published Alaska tariff or operating rate exists for winter fuel hauling — three rounds of verification confirmed this by exhausting the candidate sources (the 267-page ASTAR study contains no fuel-haul dollars; the UIC haul account publishes operations but no cost; the Noatak haul-system proposal specifies the full operation — 20–30 mi route, 3,000–5,000 gal/trip, ~200 trips/season, $425K tracked tractor — but prices nothing, deferring operating cost to "end-user pays"). The mode spans two operationally different regimes — engineered ice roads driven by conventional tankers (~$0.004/gal-mi) and tundra snow trails worked by tracked cat-trains (~$0.02/gal-mi) — and the regime traffic split is now data-backed at ≈50/50 (PCE FY2025 gallons: Nuiqsut 279,232 vs. Atqasuk 274,877), so the single recommended rate ($0.010) is a confirmed midpoint rather than a coin-flip, though it still misprices each regime by ~2× in opposite directions. The one remaining load-bearing assumption is the ~$900/hr convoy rate; a real NSB/UIC contract figure (FOIA-able) would retire it.

3. Method A / B / C — full derivations with arithmetic

Method A — Regulatory filing: bulk line-haul cost, Valdez refinery → Fairbanks (DEC report, citing Econ One)

Verified against the DEC PDF, p. 15 (§1.3): the 20 ¢ and 16 ¢ figures appear verbatim, attributed to Econ One. The Econ One source deck (Pulliam/AOGA, December 2016) was read on the second pass; its map on p. 15 ("Cost of In-State Product Movement Can Be Significant") codes the Valdez→Fairbanks 20 ¢/gal movement as TRUCK (green) and Anchorage→Fairbanks 16 ¢/gal as RAIL (red) via the legend — so the mode is now confirmed directly, not merely by geographic necessity. Vintage: DEC cites a 2015 Econ One report; the 2016 deck carries no explicit dollar-year, so the 2015→2026 CPI factor (1.409) established on the first pass is retained (a 2016 basis would give ~1.375, immaterial at this rounding).

  • Transport cost, Valdez refinery → Fairbanks (truck, confirmed by Econ One's mode legend): $0.20/gal over 364 mi (2015$ basis).
  • $0.20 ÷ 364 mi = $0.000549/gal-mi (2015$)
  • 2026$: 0.000549 × 1.409 = $0.000774/gal-mi ≈ $0.00077/gal-mi
  • Cross-modal sanity check: rail Anchorage→Fairbanks $0.16 ÷ 356 mi = $0.000449 (2015$) → $0.000633 (2026$). Rail correctly comes in below truck; truck/rail per-mile ratio 1.22 is plausible. (The Econ One map also shows a separate ≈ 2 ¢/gal pipeline Kenai→Anchorage and a ≈ 10 ¢/gal barge leg — neither is a truck movement, so both are excluded from Method A.)
  • This is a refinery-to-rack bulk movement in ~10,000–13,000-gal units — the closest published analogue to a routing-model edge cost. Includes carrier operating cost + margin; excludes rack fees and distributor margin.

Method B — Cost build-up: Alaska tanker $/mile ÷ payload

  • National anchor (ATRI 2024, verified primary tables): Specialized sector $2.32/truck-mile (Table 11, p. 17); industry average $2.260/mi (Table 8, p. 15). Tank operations fall inside the Specialized sector — ATRI publishes no separate tanker line-item — so $2.32 is the correct national starting point.
  • Alaska uplift: driver comp ≈ 1.8–2× national (wages $0.798 + benefits $0.197 = $0.995/mi of the ATRI 2024 total, Table 8 → +$0.80–1.00/mi at 1.8–2×); Alaska diesel above national average (fuel $0.481/mi → +~$0.08/mi); harsher R&M ($0.198/mi base). Implied Alaska tanker cost ≈ $2.95–$3.25/mi. Independent market evidence: Alaska freight rates $2.80–$3.10/mi (rate basis, margin included); a broker page verified by fetch (heavyequipmenttransport.com) quotes Alaska truck services at $3.50–$6.00/mi, but that is heavy-equipment/oversize-oriented and skews high — treated as corroboration that Alaska rates sit well above the national $2.26 average, not as a tanker rate. Adopt $3.00/truck-mile (2025–26$, margin included).
  • Payload: 10,500 gal central (Dalton semis ~10,000 gal; Petro Star A-trains 13,000 gal; conservative single-trailer 9,000 gal).
  • Deadhead: fuel tankers return empty → round-trip factor 1.9 applied to the one-way delivery distance.
  • Central: ($3.00 × 1.9) ÷ 10,500 = 5.70 ÷ 10,500 = $0.000543/gal-mi (≈2026$)
  • Low: ($2.80 × 1.8) ÷ 13,000 = 5.04 ÷ 13,000 = $0.000388
  • High: ($3.25 × 2.0) ÷ 9,000 = 6.50 ÷ 9,000 = $0.000722
  • Method B: $0.00054/gal-mi (range $0.00039–$0.00072), 2026$.

Finding 1 — the ocean-linehaul distance gradient is undetectable (in fact negative) in revealed PCE prices. Across the four clean ocean hubs, price falls as distance from Cook Inlet rises: Naknek (nearest, 1,225 sm) $3.86 → Kotzebue (farthest, ~1,830 sm) $3.33. A naive OLS of hub $/gal on route sm gives a slope of ≈ −7×10⁻⁴ $/gal-mi (wrong sign; R² near zero). This is not a freight rate — it is dominated by carrier/volume/competition effects (Crowley vs Delta Western vs Vitus service areas, hub throughput, and how much non-diesel generation each hub blends in — Kotzebue and Nome both run large wind fleets, shrinking and cheapening their diesel buy). The PCE cross-section therefore confirms, on margin-free data, the chapter's standing conclusion: you cannot back a positive ocean linehaul $/gal-mi out of revealed rural prices — the linehaul signal (~$0.3/gal) is swamped by the ~$3–5/gal commodity+basis floor. The engineering methods A–C remain the only sound basis for the linehaul edge.

Finding 2 — Bethel's margin-free fuel is $4.45/gal, not $6.72. The prior Method-D "Bethel $6.72" was a retail pump/heating-oil price carrying dealer margin, storage carry, and sales tax. The utility bulk price is $4.45. Against an Anchorage-area utility diesel bulk basis of roughly $3.2–3.5/gal in the same FY2025 window (Railbelt utility diesel / OPIS rack + local delivery), the true margin-free Bethel–Anchorage delivered-fuel differential is ≈ $1.0–1.3/gal, not the $2.8–3.2 the retail figures implied. The PCE differential is the net-of-everything realized price a consolidated utility (AVEC operates one blended fuel-procurement pool across ~50 villages) actually pays — AVEC socializes river-distribution cost across its network, so an individual upriver AVEC village's price barely rises with distance even though its freight cost does. This is the single cleanest confirmation in the whole derivation that Western Alaska price premia are overwhelmingly non-freight.

2. How bulk fuel moves by air in Alaska (carriers, aircraft, pricing structure)

  • Everts Air Fuel (Fairbanks International west ramp, plus Kenai) is the dominant carrier — "the primary source of fuel for most rural villages in the state" (alaska.org, "Pipeline in the Sky"). Aircraft: Douglas DC-6A/B tankers (FAA-certified internal aluminum tanks, split-load capable, 2,000–5,000 gal per Everts; 5,000–6,000 gal cited for short hauls; ISER cites "4,900-gallon DC-6") and Curtiss C-46 tankers (roughly half a DC-6 load) for shorter/rougher strips (evertsair.com/fuel, AOPA 2005). Weight, not tank volume, binds for diesel: Everts' stated average DC-6 payload is 28,000 lb ≈ ~4,000 gal of diesel (7.05 lb/gal) or ~4,700 gal of gasoline (6.1 lb/gal).
  • Alaska Air Fuel, Inc. (Wasilla) flies Douglas DC-4/C-54 tankers, delivering Jet A, 100LL, mogas and heating oil statewide since 2012, often hauling Crowley-supplied product (e.g., the 2024 fatal C-54 accident was a fuel run Fairbanks→Kobuk) (Wikipedia, alaskaairfuel.com).
  • Very small communities/lodges get drummed or small-tank fuel in Caravan/CASA-class aircraft (e.g., Glenn Air) at much higher unit cost.
  • Pricing structure: bulk fuel air service is sold on a charter basis only (Everts states this explicitly) — the customer (village corporation, city, utility, North Slope Borough, federal agency) pays for the round trip; the aircraft returns empty. Villages then retail the fuel with local tank-farm and margin costs on top. So there are two distinct "costs per gallon-mile": (a) the charter transport component and (b) the delivered retail premium (charter + local storage/handling/margin). Both are derived and kept separate.
  • The DC-6/C-46/DC-4 fleet burns 100LL avgas, a major and volatile cost input (national average $7.44/gal, Alaska region FBO average $10.97, July 2026, AirNav).
  • Air delivery is the year-round backstop: barge-served villages that miss the barge window also fly fuel in "at even higher prices" (KYUK, Apr 2026).

6. Distance-dependence analysis

Cost per gallon-mile is NOT constant — it falls hyperbolically with distance. Structurally (Method B): per-gal-mi = $0.0126 + ($2.18/D) for DC-6 loads. The fixed component (loading, pump-off, taxi, dispatch — ~1.5 hr of a ~$5,800/hr asset spread over 4,000 gal) contributes $2.18/gal per delivery regardless of distance. Consequences: per-gal-mi at 100 mi ($0.034) is ~1.9× the 400-mi value ($0.018); halving load size (C-46) raises the whole curve ~35–50%. The empirical points agree: Coal Creek (143 mi, 1,400-gal lot) ≈ $0.035 vs AKP (252 mi, full loads) ≈ $0.018–0.026 (verified values). For the routing model, a two-part edge cost (fixed per-delivery $ + true per-mile $) is strictly better than any single $/gal-mi constant. Beyond ~400–500 mi, aircraft fuel uplift begins to displace payload, so the curve flattens then rises slightly; irrelevant at typical hub–village stage lengths (100–300 mi).

1. Search strategy

The plan was: (1) establish the operational reality of winter fuel hauls on the three named route archetypes — Deadhorse→Nuiqsut engineered ice road, Utqiagvik→Atqasuk tundra snow trail (CWAT), and the Kuskokwim River ice road out of Bethel; (2) then triangulate cost with four independent evidence families: (A) engineering cost build-up from equipment/labor/speed/payload, (B) revealed retail fuel-price differentials between winter-road-served and hub communities (Alaska DCRA Fuel Price Report, GNWT community fuel prices, Ontario First Nations winter roads), (C) circumpolar heavy-haul winter-road tonne-km economics (Tibbitt–Contwoyto), and (D) documented real-world haul events as bounds (the Feb–Mar 2026 Anaktuvuk Pass emergency overland haul). Twenty searches were run against news archives (KYUK, KNBA, ADN, Alaska Beacon), state PDFs (DCRA, Alaska OMB, DOT&PF), North Slope Borough budget documents, GNWT releases, and Canadian sources, followed by the 2026-07-15 verification fetch round.

2. How fuel moves on Alaska winter roads (vehicles, loads, operators)

Three distinct operations exist, and they matter for cost:

(a) Engineered oilfield-standard ice roads (Deadhorse/Kuparuk/Alpine → Nuiqsut). A seasonal ice road of about 62 miles connects the Dalton Highway at Deadhorse to Nuiqsut (a ~31-mile segment ties Alpine/Kuparuk to the village) (dangerousroads.org; ADN 2014). These roads are built to oilfield spec and carry conventional highway tractors with tank trailers; upstream, Colville Inc. runs 54 tractors and 85 tank trailers hauling >2M gal/month up the Dalton to Prudhoe Bay (colvilleinc.com), and the same class of equipment makes the ice-road leg. Full or near-full tanker payloads (≈5,000–8,000 gal) are feasible; speeds are ice-limited (roughly 15–25 mph loaded).

(b) Tundra packed-snow trails — the NSB Community Winter Access Trail (CWAT). First established winter 2017/2018 (ASTAR TM4), with BLM right-of-way authorized January 2019 (Alaska Business, verified), the CWAT is rebuilt annually from compacted snow: reconnaissance and pre-packing begin late December, trail construction runs through January, escorted caravans run mid-February through April, then the trail is decommissioned. Verified route lengths (Alaska Business): Prudhoe Bay–Utqiaġvik 300 mi; Utqiaġvik–Atqasuk 68 mi; Utqiaġvik–Wainwright 127 mi; plus the southern route Galbraith Lake (Dalton Hwy)–Anaktuvuk Pass, the "109-mile route" of the 2026 haul. Before the CWAT, a historic winter trail on the same corridor carried Rolligon fuel hauls from Utqiaġvik to Atqasuk (ASTAR 2019, verified — the study maps a distinct "Winter Fuel Delivery Route" but quantifies nothing about it). Haul vehicles are low-ground-pressure tracked tractors (PistenBully, Tucker; a Prinoth BR350 is also documented on the trail). In the verified Feb–Mar 2026 emergency operation, UIC Oil & Gas Support delivered ~100,000 gal to Anaktuvuk Pass in 12 runs (~8,333 gal/run) using PistenBullys towing fuel trailers, with a Sherp scouting river-ice crossings (~3 ft of water), six full-time crew (~10 with rotation), staged 147 mi from the Deadhorse OGS facility; early round trips took 7–10 days, optimized to ~1.5 days by late February (uicalaska.com, verified — no cost figures published). General CWAT usage 2022–23: ~58 caravans, ~140 vehicles, treks of 3–33 hours.

(c) River ice roads (Kuskokwim). The Kuskokwim Ice Road, maintained largely by the Native Village of Napaimute's crew with state funding, runs 200–300+ miles and connects 15+ villages (~11,000 people) to Bethel (ADN 2019; DOT&PF). Fuel moves two ways: vendors (Vitus Energy, Crowley from the Bethel terminal) run tanker trucks up-river when ice permits — in thin-ice years Vitus substituted fleets of smaller fuel trucks on routes scouted by Napaimute (tribalsafety.org) — and households self-haul drums/totes by pickup. Maintenance has cost $300,000–$370,000/season (KYUK; Alaska Native News).

Who pays what: road/trail provision is publicly funded — State of Alaska for the Kuskokwim; for the CWAT, a verified FY2020 NPR-A impact grant of $1,672,077 to the NSB (OMB project 62733). Hauling itself is done by borough enterprise operations or contractors (UIC OGS, Colville, Vitus, Crowley). NSB residential heating fuel is subsidized at $1.50–2.50/gal depending on village (verified DCRA Winter 2026: AKP/Atqasuk/Nuiqsut $1.50, Point Hope $1.74, Wainwright $1.75, Kaktovik $2.50); the subsidy does not extend to businesses, so commercial prices must be used for revealed-cost inference. Utqiaġvik reports no heating-oil price: homes heat with natural gas and the vendor declined to report a commercial price in Winter 2026.

4. Method A / B / C — full derivations with arithmetic

Method A — engineering cost build-up (two route variants)

A1. Ice-road tanker (Nuiqsut / Kuskokwim type). - Base all-in truck cost: ATRI 2024 = $2.26/mi at ~40 mph effective → $2.26 × 40 = $90/hr (lower-48, 2024$). - Alaska remote-winter premium ×1.75 (short season → poor utilization, mobilization, winterization, higher wages, standby): $90 × 1.75 ≈ $158/hr ≈ $160/hr (2026$; ATRI 2024→2026 inflation is inside the 1.75 rounding). - Ice-road speeds: 15 mph loaded / 25 mph empty. Time per delivered mile (round trip) = 1/15 + 1/25 = 0.1067 hr/mi → $160 × 0.1067 = $17.1/delivered-mile. Add 20% for loading/unloading/convoy delays → $20.5/delivered-mile. - Payload: 5,000 gal typical community tanker (range 2,500 gal thin-ice small trucks → 7,500 gal full trailer on oilfield-spec ice). - Rate = $20.5 / 5,000 = $0.0041/gal-mi (range $0.0027–0.0082).

A2. Tundra snow-trail cat-train (Atqasuk / Anaktuvuk type), calibrated to the verified 2026 AKP haul (100,000 gal, 12 runs, ~8,333 gal/run, 109 mi, PistenBullys + fuel trailers, 6 crew). Physical inputs now independently corroborated (2026-07-17) by the 2012 Noatak NWFHLS proposal: single Arctic Snow Tractor + sled, 3,000–5,000 gal/trip, 10–12 mph, ~200 round trips to move 350–400k gal/yr over a 20–30-mi overland leg — the same payload/speed/single-unit architecture assumed here. Noatak states no operating cost, so the calibration below is unchanged. - Convoy cost: ~3 tracked units with operators @ ~$275/hr (typical Alaska oilfield tracked-support rates, assumption — still the only unverified input) + $75/hr supervision/support = $900/hr. Sanity check from Noatak: a one-tractor, 3–5-job, self-sustaining operation implies a crew/equipment hourly well below the 3-unit AKP convoy; the AKP-derived $900/hr therefore sits at the emergency/multi-unit end of the range, consistent with treating the routine rate as ~60% of it (below). - Trip time: verified — optimized round trips of ~1.5 days (36 hr) by late February. (The original blind assumption was 32.5 hr from 7/10 mph speeds + 6 hr load/rest; the observed 36 hr replaces it — a 10% correction.) Early trail-breaking round trips took 7–10 days (2 ft of new snow); even with idle time billed at reduced standby rates these runs cost roughly 2× per gallon, so the campaign average sits above the optimized figure. - Optimized run: $900 × 36 ≈ $32,400/run → $32,400 / 8,333 gal ≈ $3.89/gal over 109 mi → $0.036/gal-mi (emergency-campaign conditions, established trail). Campaign floor 12 × $32.4k ≈ $390k. Cross-check: airlifting 100,000 gal at typical flown-fuel freight of $3.5–5/gal would cost $350–500k, and the verified weekly airlift ceiling was ~18,500 gal — the Borough's overland choice is consistent with roughly airlift-parity cost at much higher weekly capacity. - Routine, planned haul (better utilization, no standby/urgency premium in the hourly rate): ~60% of emergency cost → ≈$0.021/gal-mi (range $0.017–0.036, upper end = emergency).

Method B — revealed retail price differentials

B2 (primary). Ontario Asheweig winter-road system (~350 km serving six First Nations; Northern Policy Institute, April 2015, verified): gasoline flown in ≈ C$2.00/L; via winter road ≈ C$1.35/L (quote verified verbatim). Road-served hub price at the time ≈ C$1.05–1.15/L (assumption — the article does not state it; Pickle Lake–area). Winter-road haul + local increment ≈ C$0.20–0.30/L, take C$0.25/L over an average ~250 km (155 mi) haul: C$0.25/L × 3.785 L/gal = C$0.946/gal ÷ 155 mi = C$0.0061/gal-mi (2015 C$) × 0.78 USD/CAD = US$0.0048 × 1.36 (CPI 2015→2026) = US$0.0065/gal-mi — and this still contains some local vendor margin, so pure haul ≈ $0.004–0.007/gal-mi.

B3 (corroboration). GNWT Délı̨nę (2026 winter-resupply-priced gasoline C$1.95/L; full-cost-recovery pricing verified verbatim: prices "recover the full laid-in cost of fuel, including wholesale purchase and transportation costs") vs. Yellowknife-area rack ≈ C$1.35/L: differential C$0.60/L over ~800 km effective haul (highway + Mackenzie Valley winter road): C$0.60 × 3.785 = C$2.27/gal ÷ (800 × 0.621 = 497 mi) = C$0.00457/gal-mi × 0.73 = US$0.0033/gal-mi — includes GNWT storage/handling, so pure haul is somewhat lower; consistent with B2 given the longer, partly-highway route.

B1′ (rebuilt on the verified DCRA table — within-borough differentials). All NSB villages carry comparable small-market tank-farm/utility overhead and the same commercial (unsubsidized) pricing regime, so comparing winter-road-served villages against the Borough's marine-served commercial baseline ($6.96–7.60: Wainwright $6.96, Kaktovik $7.50, Point Hope $7.60) nets out most of the non-transport overhead that contaminated the original B1: - Nuiqsut $7.70 (engineered ice road, 62 mi from Deadhorse): premium $0.10–0.74 → $0.002–0.012/gal-mi gross — Nuiqsut prices like a marine-served village; the ice-road tanker leg adds almost nothing. Corroborates A1. - Atqasuk $8.80 (snow trail, 68 mi from Utqiaġvik): premium $1.20–1.84 → $0.018–0.027/gal-mi gross — lands squarely on the A2 routine band. - AKP $9.97 (109-mi snow trail + flown fuel): premium $2.37–3.01 → $0.022–0.028/gal-mi gross (a snow-trail/airlift blend). Caveats: single-vendor point-in-time prices, and the upstream supply basis differs (Deadhorse trucked fuel vs. ocean barge), so these stay gross corroborations rather than point inputs. The original B1 (Deadhorse laid-in assumption chain → $0.063 gross) is retired: its $3.90 "differential" was mostly tank-farm and utility overhead that the within-borough comparison removes.

Method C — circumpolar heavy-haul tonne-km economics (Tibbitt–Contwoyto)

Verified inputs: annual operating budget C$21–22M (JV, via NWT Geoscience Forum); yearly average 8,336 loads / 281,363 tons; ~400 km; season ~67 days. - Road provision: C$21.5M ÷ 281,363 t ≈ C$76/t; average haul ≈ 350 km → C$0.22/t-km road share. - Line-haul trucking on top: C$0.15–0.25/t-km (ice-speed truckload work) → all-in ≈ C$0.37–0.47/t-km; adopt C$0.42/t-km central. (The "<C$0.10/t-km" claim found for winter-road trucking evidently excludes road provision; rejected as a central value, retained as a floor.) - Convert for diesel: C$0.42 ÷ (313 gal/t × 0.621 mi/km) = C$0.42/194.4 = C$0.00216/gal-mi × 0.73 = US$0.0016/gal-mi (range US$0.0014–0.0018; floor from the C$0.10 claim ≈ US$0.0004). (Pre-verification value was $0.0017 off the C$25M snippet — a 6% shift.) - Interpretation: this is the industrial-scale floor — 6,000–10,000 loads amortizing one road. Community hauls (tens of loads) cannot reach this efficiency.

Method D — documented event bound (emergency)

The verified Feb–Mar 2026 Anaktuvuk Pass operation (Method A2's calibration event) implies ≈ $0.036/gal-mi under emergency conditions on an established trail (36-hr round trips), and materially more during the trail-breaking weeks (7–10-day round trips); the airlift alternative it supplemented (Everts Air Cargo, 18,500 gal flown in a week) sits at roughly $0.03–0.05/gal-mi-equivalent. Labeled emergency bound, not routine.

6. Route-type differences

Costs differ systematically by roughly an order of magnitude across route types:

Route type Vehicle / payload Speed / tempo 2026 USD $/gal-mi
Industrial engineered ice road (Tibbitt-scale, thousands of loads) Highway tanker, full loads 15–25 mph 0.001–0.002 (floor)
Community engineered/river ice road (Nuiqsut, Kuskokwim) Tanker truck, 2,500–7,500 gal 15–25 mph 0.003–0.008
Tundra packed-snow trail (CWAT: Atqasuk 68 mi, AKP 109 mi) Tracked tractor + fuel trailers, ~2,500–3,000 gal/unit 5–10 mph; 36-hr optimized round trip on the 109-mi AKP run (verified) 0.017–0.036 (upper end = emergency)

The driver is payload × speed: a cat-train moves roughly one-third the fuel at one-third the speed of an ice-road tanker, for a similar-or-higher hourly cost. The verified within-borough retail premiums (§4 B1′) reproduce the same order-of-magnitude split: Nuiqsut +$0.10–0.74 over marine-served villages vs. Atqasuk +$1.20–1.84 and AKP +$2.37–3.01.

7. Cross-method comparison and reconciliation

  • A1 (build-up, ice-road tanker): $0.0041 (0.0027–0.0082) — independent of any price data.
  • B1′ (verified within-NSB differential, Nuiqsut): $0.002–0.012 gross — an ice-road-served village prices almost identically to marine-served villages; converges with A1.
  • B2 (Ontario revealed differential, corrected to 2015 basis): $0.0065, with margin contamination pushing the pure-haul figure toward $0.004–0.007 — converges with A1.
  • B3 (NWT Délı̨nę): $0.0033 — consistent, given partial highway routing.
  • C (Tibbitt tonne-km, verified budget): $0.0014–0.0018, central $0.0016 — deliberately below the community band; explains itself via scale (8,000+ loads vs. dozens), so no conflict.
  • A2/D (snow trail, recalibrated on the verified 36-hr round trip): $0.017–0.036, routine ≈ $0.021 — an order above the tanker band, and now directly corroborated by the verified within-borough premiums (Atqasuk $0.018–0.027, AKP $0.022–0.028 gross), which land inside the A2 band rather than merely bounding it.

Three genuinely independent families (engineering build-up; revealed price differentials in two countries — now including a clean within-borough comparison; heavy-haul tonne-km economics) bracket the community ice-road tanker cost at $0.003–0.008/gal-mi and the snow-trail cat-train at $0.017–0.03/gal-mi. A single mode rate must sit between them, weighted by the gallon traffic split between the tanker-served (engineered ice road) and sled-served (snow trail) regimes.

Traffic weighting — now anchored on PCE gallons (2026-07-17), previously an equal-weight assumption. The two Alaska communities whose fuel actually moves on winter overland routes in the model's ice_road mode are Nuiqsut (engineered ice road, tanker regime) and Atqasuk (packed-snow trail, sled regime). The NSB budget publishes no gallons, but the PCE FY2025 report gives per-community power-generation diesel: Nuiqsut 279,232 gal vs. Atqasuk 274,877 gal — essentially 50/50. Total road-delivered throughput (heating + vehicle + power) tilts slightly toward the tanker side: Nuiqsut's population (500 vs. 307), NSB fuel-operation budget ($571k vs. $475k), and larger tank farm all point to modestly more gallons on the ice-road/tanker regime, partly offset by Nuiqsut having shifted much of its power generation to natural gas (so less liquid fuel per capita). Anaktuvuk Pass (320,380 gal) is deliberately excluded from the weighting — despite the dramatic 2026 emergency overland haul, AKP is resupplied primarily by air, not by a standing winter road, so its gallons are not sled-regime traffic (they remain the §4-D emergency bound). Net: the real gallon-weighted split is ~50/50, tilting to perhaps 55/45 in favor of the cheaper tanker regime — which confirms the prior equal-weight assumption rather than moving it, and if anything nudges the point estimate very slightly downward.

  • Equal weight (50/50): arithmetic mean of 0.0045 and 0.021 ≈ 0.013; geometric mean ≈ √(0.0045 × 0.021) ≈ 0.0097.
  • Tanker-tilted (55/45): arithmetic ≈ 0.055×0.021 rescaled = 0.45×0.021 + 0.55×0.0045 ≈ 0.0119; geometric ≈ 0.0045^0.55 × 0.021^0.45 ≈ 0.0092.

The report takes $0.010/gal-mi, leaning geometric because the high-end (sled) figure carries residual emergency contamination. The PCE-anchored weights leave both means straddling 0.010, so the point estimate is unchanged; what changed is its status — the tanker-vs-sled mix is now data-backed (§9 item 2 resolved) rather than a coin-flip, raising confidence in the point value from low-to-moderate to moderate. (Pre-verification means were 0.012/0.0092 → same rounded point value.)

Recommended rate: $0.010 per gallon-mile (2026 USD) — unchanged by the verification round. - Range: $0.004 (engineered ice road, full tanker loads, routine season) to $0.027 (tundra snow-trail cat-train, difficult conditions). Absolute floor for industrial-scale operations: ~$0.001–0.002. Emergency snow-trail operations run ≈ $0.036 (verified-calibrated) and are excluded from the range. - Confidence: Moderate-to-high in the range — both sub-bands are now corroborated by verified within-borough price differentials independent of the engineering assumptions, and A2's payload/speed/architecture are independently confirmed by the 2012 Noatak NWFHLS proposal. Trail-provision adder, re-derived on the now-read OMB sheet: CWAT is a $4.0M total award (FY2019 supp + FY2020 $1,672,077) over 07/2019–06/2024 ≈ $800k/yr for the ~500–600-trail-mile NPR-A network, and the sheet's scope is trail maintenance + traveler safety, not fuel-haul funding (haulers use the trail incidentally). Also excluded: tank-farm storage and retail markup; and emergency premiums (≈$0.036 overland; displaced airlift ~$0.03–0.05).

10. Verification addendum — second pass (local primary documents, 2026-07-17)

Four downloaded primary PDFs were read to test whether any contained the winter fuel-haul cost data the chapter had been missing. Summary of what each contributed and the net effect on the estimate.

(1) Northwest Arctic Borough — Noatak Winter Fuel Haul System (NWFHLS), 2012 legislative grant proposal (TPS Report 58673v1) — the headline document. This is a real Alaska overland-fuel-haul engineering proposal, and it is the closest primary source to the chapter's Method A2. What it gives: - Capital: $425,000 — $350,000 to procure one Arctic Snow Track Tractor + sled "capable of hauling fuel off road for up to 30 miles in winter conditions," $75,000 for permitting/environmental/logistics planning. (Matches the project's older $425k research figure exactly, and independently confirms the ~$425k Arctic-tractor capital cost.) - Route: a 20–30-mile overland (tundra) winter road from an access point on the DMTS/Red Dog haul road to Noatak; three candidate corridors. - Vehicle performance: single Arctic Snow Tractor, 10–12 mph, ~2-hr one-way trip, 3,000–5,000 gal/trip. - System scale: Noatak annual fuel demand 350,000–400,000 gal/yr (heating + power + vehicular); ~200 round trips/season with one tractor; 3–5 seasonal jobs; dormant in summer. - Supply chain: barge to Red Dog/DMTS port (Jul–Sep) → NANA-Lynden truck up the DMTS road → transfer to snow tractor for the final 20–30-mi overland leg. - Baseline it displaces: Noatak is 100% air-served (AVEC/school charters), retail fuel >$10/gal, "highest cost of living in the nation."

Critical negative result: the proposal explicitly structures the haul as a "self-sustaining business — operational costs will be paid for by the end-user" and gives no operating cost per hour, per trip, per gallon, or per gallon-mile, and no projected $/gal savings figure (the "savings" case is qualitative: economies of scale + money staying in the community). So it corroborates every physical input to Method A2 (payload band, tracked speed, single-unit architecture, trips-per-season order of magnitude) and adds a third real Alaska route length (20–30 mi, alongside AKP's 109 mi and Atqasuk's 68 mi), but it does not replace A2's assumed ~$900/hr convoy rate. That assumption remains the single load-bearing unverified input in the whole chapter.
